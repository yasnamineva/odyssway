A glucose meter, a hearing aid or a box of contact lenses doesn't feel like something
customs cares about. In some countries it is. A medical device is a regulated product,
and some countries set limits on how many you can bring, or ask for approval first.

Here is what the official rules say in the countries we've checked so far. These are
**customs and import** rules. Airline and airport security rules (for example, carrying
a device through screening) are a separate question.

## Singapore: approval first, unless the device is already in use

Singapore is the strictest of these. To bring a medical device for personal use, you
need approval from the Health Sciences Authority (HSA) **at least 10 working days before
you arrive**. There's an exception: devices already in use when you arrive, such as an
implanted device or one already in contact with your skin or eyes.

- Each approval covers at most **3 months' supply**.
- Some devices can't be brought in this way at all: HIV test kits, hazardous
  radiation-emitting devices, and devices meant to be administered by a qualified
  practitioner.
- If you're only transiting and stay in the transit zones, you don't need approval.

[Singapore's rules →](/destinations/singapore#medical-devices-sg)

## Malaysia: no permit, but one of each

Malaysia exempts personal-use medical devices from registration and from the customs
import permit. The limits depend on the type of device:

- **1 piece of each type** of low- or medium-risk device, such as a glucose monitoring
  device, a hearing aid or an in-vitro diagnostic product.
- **Up to 3 months' supply** of consumables, such as contact lenses and test strips.

The devices must be for you or your immediate family, keep their original labels, and
the Medical Device Authority may ask for a prescription or a letter from a healthcare
professional.

[Malaysia's rules →](/destinations/malaysia#medical-devices-my)

## Taiwan: a list of items, each with a cap

Taiwan's Food and Drug Administration lists the devices travellers can bring without
special approval. Each category is allowed once every 6 months. For example:

- daily disposable contact lenses: 60 per diopter, one brand and two diopters per person;
- corrective lenses: 1 pair;
- medical masks: up to 250.

The page doesn't say how other devices are treated. Devices brought in this way can't
be sold or given away; selling them can lead to a fine of NT$30,000 to NT$1,000,000.

[Taiwan's rules →](/destinations/taiwan#medical-devices-tw)

## Thailand: bring the paperwork

Thailand allows a medical device in the amount you need for personal use, with three
documents:

1. information about the device, such as its trade name, label, specifications and
   manufacturer;
2. a letter explaining its purpose and confirming it won't be sold;
3. a medical certificate confirming you have a condition that needs it.

[Thailand's rules →](/destinations/thailand#medical-devices-th)

## Japan: one unit

Japan Customs allows **one unit** of medical equipment for home use, such as an electric
massager or a low-frequency therapy device, and disposable contact lenses for up to
**two months**. For anything beyond that, Japan Customs refers you to a pharmaceutical
inspector at a Regional Bureau of Health and Welfare.

[Japan's rules →](/destinations/japan#medical-devices-jp)

## Ecuador: duty-free up to two of each meter

Ecuador's customs list lets each traveller or family group bring up to **2 each** of
blood-pressure monitors, thermometers and glucose meters duty-free. Wheelchairs,
crutches and similar aids are allowed in personal-use quantities.

[Ecuador's rules →](/destinations/ecuador#medical-devices-ec)

## What we haven't found yet

None of these official pages names CPAP machines or insulin pumps specifically. If you
travel with one, check with the destination's health authority before you go. We'll add more countries as we verify
them. Search your device in [Trip Check](/trip-check) to see the current rule.
