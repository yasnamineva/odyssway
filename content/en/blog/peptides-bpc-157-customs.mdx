Peptides such as BPC-157, TB-500 and melanotan II are easy to buy online,
often as small vials marked **"research use only"**. At the border, that
label doesn't protect you, and in three of the four countries below the
vials themselves are the problem.

The short version: **an unapproved peptide is treated as an unapproved
medicine**, not as a supplement or a lab chemical. Growth hormone and HCG
prescribed to you are a separate case, with clearer rules.

## "Research use only" doesn't count

All three regulators we checked say the label makes no legal difference:

- **United States.** In a March 2026 warning letter to a peptide seller, the
  FDA said that, despite "Research Use Only" labels, the products were drugs
  intended for human use.
- **Canada.** Health Canada says this kind of labelling "does not make these
  products legal or exempt from regulatory requirements."
- **New Zealand.** Medsafe says the wording "has no legal effect."

## Country by country

**United States: not allowed.** BPC-157, TB-500, melanotan II and similar
peptides are not FDA-approved. The FDA's rule is that importing an unapproved
drug is illegal, even for personal use and even if another country allows
it. The FDA *may* let a small personal supply through in narrow cases, such
as a serious condition with no treatment available in the US. That is a
choice the FDA makes, not something you can count on, and the FDA lists
safety concerns for most of these peptides.

**Canada: not allowed.** Health Canada says peptides are generally regulated
as prescription drugs, and unauthorized drug products are illegal. Its April
2026 advisory names BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, GHK-Cu, melanotan
and retatrutide among products it has seized. It also works with border
officers to stop unauthorized shipments. An authorized drug has an
eight-digit DIN on its label. Research vials don't.

**New Zealand: only with a prescription.** Medsafe says many peptides are
prescription medicines, and it's unlawful to import or possess them without
authorisation. If a doctor has prescribed one to you, the normal traveller
rule applies:

- no more than **3 months' supply**,
- a copy of the prescription or a letter from your doctor,
- the original containers,
- and **declare it** on your arrival card.

Having peptides posted to you in New Zealand is stricter: you need a
prescription from a New Zealand prescriber.

**United Kingdom: growth hormone and HCG, carried in person.** UK law lists
growth hormone (somatropin) and HCG as controlled drugs, but it allows you to
bring them **in person, for your own use**. Keep them in your hand luggage,
bring no more than 3 months' supply, and if you don't live in the UK, carry a
letter signed by your prescriber. Having them posted to you isn't covered,
and gov.uk says posted medicine will be taken away. We haven't verified a UK
rule on BPC-157 or other research peptides yet.

## What this means for your trip

If your peptide came from a "research" website, plan on **not packing it**
for the US, Canada or New Zealand. If a doctor prescribed it, bring the
prescription and stay within the supply limit.

Ozempic, Wegovy and Mounjaro are peptides too, but they're approved medicines
with their own, easier rules. See
[flying with Ozempic, Wegovy or Mounjaro](/blog/ozempic-wegovy-mounjaro-travel).
