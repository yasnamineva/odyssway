The Entry/Exit System (EES) is no longer a thing to prepare for at these
five borders — it's live. France, Spain, Germany, Italy, and the
Netherlands are now recording every non-EU entry and exit digitally,
automatically, at the point it happens.

## What actually changed

Before EES, your passport got a stamp. A border officer's read of that stamp
— sometimes smudged, sometimes missing entirely — was the practical evidence
of when you entered and left. Under EES, your entry and exit are logged
directly: fingerprints and a facial image on your first crossing, then an
automatic match on every one after. There's no stamp to lose, misread, or
argue about.

That sounds like a small procedural swap. It isn't, for one specific reason:

## The 90/180 count is no longer generous by omission

A rounding error or a missed stamp used to work in a traveler's favor more
often than it worked against them — border officers weren't auditing your
day count against a spreadsheet. Now the system is the spreadsheet. If
you've been mentally rounding your Schengen days "close enough," that
margin is gone. Overstays get flagged automatically, not just noticed if
you happen to get an especially careful officer on your way out.

## What this doesn't change

EES doesn't change the rule itself — you can still stay up to 90 days in any
rolling 180-day period, computed exactly the same way it always has been
under the Schengen Borders Code. What changed is enforcement precision, not
the underlying math.

## What to actually do differently

Know your exact day count before you travel, not an estimate. Our
[calculator](/calculator) does the rolling-window math the same way the
official EU tool does — worth running before your next trip if you've been
tracking days in your head or on a napkin.
