# EES at the border: what to expect

> **The short answer:** no more passport stamps. On your first EES crossing
> your passport data, **fingerprints and a facial image** are registered; on
> later trips the system verifies you against that record. Each entry and exit
> is logged with its **date and place**, and overstays are flagged
> automatically.

## Your first crossing after EES

The EES registers your name, your travel document data, and your biometrics —
fingerprints and a captured facial image. This enrolment happens at the border
of whichever of the 29 participating countries you cross first. Because
enrolment involves more steps than the old stamp, first-time crossings take
longer than you may be used to.

## Every crossing after that

Your record already exists, so the border check verifies you against it and
logs the date and place of your entry or exit. The European Commission notes
the EES enables wider use of **automated border control and self-service
systems** — expect kiosks and e-gates at larger crossings.

## What the system does with your record

- Tracks your entries and exits against the [90/180-day rule](/rules/90-180-rule)
  — overstays are **detected automatically**
- Records refusals of entry
- Helps border authorities identify travellers using fake or forged documents

## Practical implications

- **Count your days before you travel** — the system counts them digitally
  either way. Our [calculator](/calculator) matches the EU's official
  methodology.
- **Your record can contain errors** (for example an exit that failed to
  register). The EES Regulation is the legal basis for accessing and
  correcting your data; per-country guides on how to do that are in
  preparation.
- EES applies to **non-EU nationals on short stays** in the 29 participating
  countries.
